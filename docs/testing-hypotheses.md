# Testing Hypotheses

## Research goal

The prototype exists to determine whether Left You One creates a compelling new social gesture, not merely whether people can operate the interface or say that the idea is nice.

The central evidence chain is:

> understands enough to open → enjoys discovery → a real person comes to mind → wants to act → recipient feels specifically thought of → the object remains interesting after opening

Breaks early in this chain matter more than enthusiasm for later features.

## Highest-priority hypotheses

### H1 — Reverse gifting is understandable through experience

**Hypothesis:** A visitor can experience the landing sequence and explain the basic inversion without a verbal pitch.

**Look for:** They describe finding something first and then thinking of a person. They do not call it a quote generator, card shop, mystery box, or random gift store.

**Failure signal:** The facilitator has to explain the premise, or the visitor believes they should choose a recipient before opening.

### H2 — Discovery is appealing without feeling like gambling

**Hypothesis:** Choosing a broad direction and opening an unknown exact gift creates healthy curiosity.

**Look for:** The participant wants to open, attends to the reveal, and can describe why the result feels authored or particular.

**Failure signal:** They expect rarity, want to reroll immediately, treat the result as a prize, or feel the reveal is arbitrary.

### H3 — A specific person genuinely comes to mind

**Hypothesis:** At least some gifts reliably evoke an unprompted association with a real person.

**Look for:** A name or recognizable relationship appears quickly, with a concrete reason: “This is Anna because she…” Different gift tones evoke different people.

**Failure signal:** Participants choose someone only because the interface demands it, give a generic answer such as “any friend,” or enjoy the words but think of nobody.

This is the concept's most important test. Treat “nobody” as high-quality evidence.

### H4 — The gift feels more substantial than a quote or link

**Hypothesis:** Authored content, visual treatment, and the act of leaving it together create an object-like gesture.

**Look for:** Senders talk about “giving,” “leaving,” “keeping,” or the intended recipient's reaction rather than copying the text into a message.

**Failure signal:** They would screenshot it, copy the sentence, or send the page as interchangeable content because the surrounding object adds no value.

### H5 — Intention makes the approximate $2 price feel natural

**Hypothesis:** After a specific person comes to mind, “Leave this for Maria · $2” feels like a trivial cost for creating the moment.

**Look for:** The price does not surprise them, and their evaluation centers on Maria's likely experience rather than the amount of text.

**Failure signal:** They ask why words cost $2, compare it to free quotes or GIFs, or lose intention when the commitment appears.

Test timing as well as amount. Showing price before association answers a different question and can turn the experience into shopping.

### H6 — Sending feels meaningfully intentional

**Hypothesis:** The sender feels more anticipation than they would after forwarding ordinary content.

**Look for:** They care about how and when the recipient opens it, want to personalize it lightly, or express curiosity about the response.

**Failure signal:** Handoff feels like copying any URL, or the optional note carries all the meaning while the gift itself feels incidental.

### H7 — Arrival communicates personal recognition and trust

**Hypothesis:** Before opening, the recipient understands that a known person encountered something and chose them specifically.

**Look for:** They can say who sent it and why they believe it was meant for them; curiosity outweighs concern about an ad, scam, or automated campaign.

**Failure signal:** They think it is marketing, a generic greeting, an automated notification, or an unsafe mystery link.

### H8 — Opening creates a small emotional event

**Hypothesis:** The recipient's deliberate reveal creates a smile, surprise, recognition, relief, or other small felt response.

**Look for:** A visible reaction, an unprompted comment about the sender or relevance, or a desire to linger.

**Failure signal:** They scan the text and immediately look for the next button, the motion feels theatrical, or the presentation resembles a stock quote card.

### H9 — “It’s with you now” creates gentle possession

**Hypothesis:** The recipient understands that this same object remains with them without interpreting it as a tradable digital asset.

**Look for:** Keeping it feels valid, and the participant can explain in plain language what being its holder means.

**Failure signal:** They ask what they own, expect a collection/profile, or believe they must pass it immediately.

### H10 — Journey adds curiosity without obligation

**Hypothesis:** Provenance makes the gift more interesting and creates a reason to return.

**Look for:** Senders and holders want to know where the same gift later went while accepting that it might never move.

**Failure signal:** Journey is irrelevant, feels invasive, or produces chain-letter pressure and performance anxiety.

### H11 — Passing can arise naturally later

**Hypothesis:** A holder can imagine a real future moment when the gift would evoke somebody else.

**Look for:** They name a plausible person or occasion without being pushed to pass now and understand that the original gift remains unchanged.

**Failure signal:** Passing feels like regifting, losing something, completing a task, or helping the product grow.

## Secondary future hypothesis

### H12 — A company can originate gifts without turning them into ads

**Hypothesis:** A company or creator can fund an origin in a way that feels like a gift to carry, with quiet provenance rather than campaign content.

**Look for:** Potential sponsors value participation and story; recipients still experience a human-scale object.

**Failure signal:** Branding dominates, recipients interpret it as promotional merchandise, or the company expects feed-style reach and conventional engagement metrics.

Do not build company features to test this initially. Concept conversations and consumer evidence come first.

## Biggest concept risks

1. **No association:** Gifts are pleasant but do not make a specific person come to mind.
2. **Quote problem:** The content feels generic, generated, or separable from the object.
3. **Value collapse:** Once price appears, users feel they are buying text.
4. **Recipient mistrust:** A sealed link from a new domain feels like advertising or a scam.
5. **Presentation overreach:** Ceremony feels slow, precious, or emotionally manipulative.
6. **Random-reward framing:** Discovery encourages rerolling or rarity expectations.
7. **Journey pressure:** Passing turns possession into obligation or a chain game.
8. **Regifting conflict:** Passing onward feels like rejecting the sender's gesture or giving away a personal keepsake.
9. **Novelty decay:** The first opening is interesting, but the experience has no repeat or return value.
10. **Content economics:** Producing enough distinct, high-quality gifts and visual treatments may be harder than the price supports.

## Suggested testing sequence

### Round 1 — Premise and association

Use a lightweight landing/reveal prototype with several deliberately different gifts. Do not explain the concept in advance. Test H1–H4 before investing in persistence.

### Round 2 — Sender intention and value

Add name, optional note, the simulated $2 moment, and a believable handoff. Test H5–H6. Ask participants to use a person who genuinely came to mind rather than role-playing a supplied recipient.

### Round 3 — Real recipient opening

Have senders hand the experience to actual people when consent and prototype safety allow. Observe recipients separately where possible so the sender does not explain the experience for them. Test H7–H9.

### Round 4 — Time and journey

Let gifts remain with recipients and follow up later. Show or simulate history only after the initial opening has been evaluated. Test H10–H11. A same-session request to pass cannot establish that somebody naturally came to mind later.

### Round 5 — Company horizon

Only after the consumer gesture shows promise, use concept material and emerging behavior with a small number of company or creator conversations. Test H12 before building campaign tooling.

Use small samples to identify repeated patterns, then broaden only when the prototype and question are stable. Do not treat compliments from collaborators as validation.

## Session method

- Begin with context such as “Please use this as you normally would,” not an explanation of the idea.
- Ask the participant to think aloud only if it does not overwhelm the quiet experience; retrospective questions may preserve the reveal better.
- Record what they do, say, and hesitate over before asking for opinions.
- Never require them to name a private relationship or share a personal note with the research team.
- Distinguish observed behavior from the participant's prediction of future behavior.
- Capture the exact gift and treatment shown; content quality is a major variable.
- Rotate gift tones and order to avoid learning from one unusually strong or weak example.
- Make clear that payment is simulated before a participant could mistake the action for a charge.
- For recipient tests, confirm that opening the link and any journey visibility are safe and consensual.

## Questions that do not lead the participant

After the landing sequence:

- “What do you think this is?”
- “What did you expect before you opened it?”
- “What, if anything, happened when you saw it?”

After discovery:

- “Tell me about the first person or situation that came to mind, if any.”
- “What made this fit or not fit them?”
- “What would you do next if this page did not ask you?”

At the commitment moment:

- “What do you think will happen if you press this?”
- “How does this amount change what you want to do?”
- “What do you feel you would be paying for?”

After recipient opening:

- “Before you opened it, what did you think had been sent?”
- “Why do you think the sender chose you?”
- “What is this now?”
- “What, if anything, would you want to do with it?”

After journey exposure:

- “What does this history add or take away?”
- “Who should be able to see it?”
- “What do you think ‘with you’ means?”
- “Under what circumstances would you pass it on?”

Avoid “Did you like it?”, “Was it special?”, and “Would you pay $2?” as primary questions. They encourage polite, hypothetical answers. Ask about meaning, expected behavior, and tradeoffs first.

## Evidence record

For each session, record:

- participant context and whether they were sender or recipient;
- prototype version, gift content, and visual treatment;
- comprehension in the participant's own words;
- whether a person came to mind and how spontaneously;
- observed emotional and interaction reactions;
- interpretation of the $2 moment;
- recipient trust and interpretation;
- interpretation of keeping, possession, journey, and passing;
- notable quotes, paraphrased when privacy requires it;
- facilitator interventions;
- surprises, failures, and follow-up questions.

Do not build an elaborate analytics pipeline at this stage. A consistent research note and a few deliberately chosen state observations are enough until the flow stabilizes.

## Decision rules

Advance technical investment when the central behaviors repeat across more than one gift, treatment, and relationship—not merely when participants praise the idea.

Rework content if discovery is enjoyable but association is vague. Rework framing if participants call it a quote or card. Rework the recipient arrival if trust or personal recognition fails. Reconsider the price, presentation, or premise if intention repeatedly collapses at commitment. Reconsider journey emphasis if keeping and passing introduce obligation rather than meaning.

The concept remains unvalidated until real sender-recipient pairs have experienced it. Mark hypotheses in `docs/prototype-progress.md` as supported, weakened, or unresolved; never “proven” from a small prototype study.
